The problem

Sizing a workforce is still a logistics project.

Every way of collecting sizes today trades cost against reliability — and a wrong size costs the same twice: once to ship it, and again to swap it.

Fitting days

Fitters, size sets and shift rotas

Sending fitters and sample sizes to every site means travel, scheduling around shifts, and the staff who are off that day — then doing it again for every new starter and every contract renewal.

Self-reported sizes

A guess, multiplied by the workforce

Asking wearers for their size is cheap but unreliable. Sizes differ between brands and garments, and a wrong guess comes back as an exchange, a second shipment, and a new starter still waiting for kit.

Tape measures

Every measurer measures differently

A review of waist-circumference studies reported measurement error from 0.7 cm to as much as 15 cm, with trained measurers at the lower end.1 Across many fitters and sites, the same body can be recorded several different ways.

FAQs

Uniform sizing questions

How does remote uniform sizing work?

The supplier or employer sends each wearer a scan link, or embeds the scan in its ordering portal. The wearer takes a front and a side photo with any smartphone, in everyday work clothes, and the measurements your size chart uses come back in seconds. Your ordering system turns them into sizes; nobody has to travel to a fitting.

Do employees need to change clothes or install anything?

No. Fitted clothing gives the best results, but everyday work clothes are fine — wearers only take off coats and bulky layers. The scan runs in a mobile browser or inside your own app, so there is nothing to install, and any smartphone camera works.

How accurate is it?

On average, measurements are within 1.6 cm (⅝ in) in everyday clothing, across 18 standard measurements, tested on people photographed in varied clothing and compared with 3D body scans. For context, reliability studies of trained measurers using a tape report waist measurement errors of 1.3–6.5 cm, and the body itself changes through the day. Every wearer is measured the same way, so results don't depend on which fitter holds the tape. The best test is your own: run a pilot with a group of wearers and compare the sizes with your current process.

How do the measurements become uniform sizes?

Measurements arrive as structured data, and your ordering system maps them to your size charts and garment rules — the same way it would use tape measurements. Wearers who fall outside the chart can be flagged for made-to-measure. Measurements describe the body, not a size, so the same scan works across every garment and manufacturer in the contract.

Can we use our own measurement definitions?

Yes. Build a custom measurement profile in the live demo — any circumference or length between points on the body — and send it to us as JSON. Your partner code then returns exactly that set on every scan, named the way your patterns are.

What happens to employee photos and personal data?

Photos are processed in memory and deleted once the result is returned; they are never stored. A scan needs no name, email or employee number: your system keeps its own wearer reference and attaches the measurements when they come back. See the data handling details.

Does it work for women's fits and different body types?

The scan reconstructs each wearer's own body shape, and the model was trained on images spanning body types, skin tones, clothing and backgrounds. Because it returns measurements rather than a size, your chart decides the fit — women's cuts, extended sizes and made-to-measure included.

How can we try it?

Start with the free live demo: two photos return measurements and a 3D body model in seconds. For a pilot, we set up your measurement set and a scan link for a group of wearers, so you can compare the results with your current sizing process.
